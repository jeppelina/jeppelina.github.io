Six common reasons things correlate

# Reason Why that shows up as a correlation
1 X causes Y Change X and Y follows, so the two move together
2 Y causes X Change Y and X follows, so the two move together
3 Something else causes both A third thing moves both at once
4 Who is in the data? Selection into the sample leaves a pattern among those included
5 How it was measured The measurements move together even when they in reality do not
6 Chance, or too small to matter In any finite sample, two unrelated things still show something

From correlation to a line

The limits of a correlation

  • No units. r = 0.38 has no straight forward interpretation. It cannot say how many points an extra hour is worth.
  • The same both ways round. The correlation of hours with score is the same number as the correlation of score with hours.

Regression as prediction

  • What is prediction?
  • A regression is a predictive model.
  • Give it an \(x\), and it hands back a prediction of \(y\).
  • It will do that more or less well.

What we call them

  • The thing we are predicting is the outcome, often called the dependent variable. We write it \(y\)
  • What we predict it from is a predictor, often called an independent variable. We write it \(x\)
  • Here \(y\) is the exam score, and \(x\) is hours studied

Regression as a straight line

The straight line equation

\[ y = a + b \cdot x \]

  • \(a\) is the intercept: where the line crosses at \(x = 0\)
  • \(b\) is the slope: how much \(y\) moves when \(x\) goes up by one

A simple example

\[ y = 0 + 1 \cdot x \]

  • If \(x = 2\), then \(y = ?\)
  • If \(x = 10\), then \(y = ?\)

Another

\[ y = 21 + 2 \cdot x \]

  • If \(x = 0\), then \(y = ?\)
  • If \(x = 10\), then \(y = ?\)

Moving the line

y = + × x

Moving the line on the data

ŷ = + × hours studied

Regression as an equation

\[ \widehat{\text{exam score}} = 100.2 + 0.35 \cdot \text{hours studied} \]

  • We can now predict the exam score if we know how many hours a student studied.
  • Someone who studied 20 hours: predicted 107.2
  • One additional hour of study: 0.35 points

The regression result

This is a predictive model, fitted on our data. Give it a number of hours and it hands back a predicted exam score.

With these 500 students, and using nothing but hours studied, no straight line predicts better than this one.

It is the same equation

\[ \widehat{\text{exam score}} = 100.2 + 0.35 \cdot \text{hours studied} \]

(Intercept) is the \(a\) from before and hours_studied is the \(b\). R has handed back the straight line, and that is what we call the regression result.

Interpreting the coefficient

Write it like this

A one-hour increase in hours studied is associated with an average change of 0.35 points in exam score.

Another phrasing

Comparing two students who differ by one hour of study, the one who studied more is predicted to score 0.35 points higher, on average.

The parts of that sentence

  • a one-hour increase says how far \(x\) moves. One hour, not one unnamed unit
  • an average change says it is about the whole group. No one student is promised 0.35 points
  • is associated with says we are not claiming the studying caused it

This is the single place where most marks are lost on the exam. Learn the wording now, while the model is simple.

The general form

A one-[unit] increase in [x] is associated with an average change of [b] [units of y] in [y].

Fill in the brackets and you have interpreted any OLS coefficient.

Caution

We will add more to this general form when we have more than one predictor, but the first part stays the same.

Have we proved that studying more leads to higher exam scores?
