How muscle grows, remembers and recovers — from the molecule to the child.

We are a clinical muscle-physiology group studying the molecular machinery of skeletal muscle adaptation: how mechanical load builds new ribosomes, how muscle keeps a memory of past training, and how this biology shapes movement in children with cerebral palsy.

Research programme

Four questions, one tissue

Skeletal muscle is remarkably plastic: it grows under load, wastes in disease, and carries traces of its history. We work across the whole arc — from the transcription of ribosomal DNA to a child crossing a finish line.

Ribosome biogenesis

To build muscle, you first have to build the factories that build muscle.

Mechanical loading switches on an RNA-Polymerase-I programme (the Pol I / c-Myc / UBF axis) that transcribes ribosomal DNA and assembles new ribosomes. We study this translational-capacity step as a rate-limiting control point for hypertrophy.

Pol I regulonrRNAhypertrophy

Muscle memory

Muscle that has trained before grows back faster. We ask why — and where the memory lives.

Epigenetic marks (DNA methylation) and structural adaptations appear to record prior training. Understanding this molecular memory reframes how we think about detraining, rehabilitation and lifelong activity.

epigeneticsmethylationdetraining

Muscle in cerebral palsy

In children with cerebral palsy, muscle becomes short and stiff. We study the biology behind contracture.

Contracted muscle shows extracellular-matrix expansion, pro-inflammatory signalling and reduced ribosomal-RNA synthesis. Mapping this pathophysiology is the first step toward better, muscle-targeted treatment.

contractureECMpaediatric

Inclusive exercise

Everyone deserves to feel like an athlete. We measure — and expand — what movement is possible.

Working with frame running and field-based cardiopulmonary testing, we quantify exercise capacity in people with motor impairments and partner with sports clubs to widen participation across the lifespan.

How it works

From a strand of DNA to a bigger muscle

A muscle cell can only make protein as fast as its ribosomes allow. When you load a muscle, it responds by building more ribosomes. Here is that pathway, one step at a time.

STEP 01
🏋️

Mechanical load

Resistance exercise stresses the muscle fibre and triggers growth signalling.

→
STEP 02
🧬

Pol I switches on

The c-Myc / UBF programme drives RNA Polymerase I to transcribe ribosomal DNA.

→
STEP 03
📜

45S pre-rRNA

A large rRNA precursor is made and processed — the raw material of new ribosomes.

→
STEP 04
⚙️

Ribosomes assemble

Translational capacity rises: the cell now has more machinery to make protein.

→
STEP 05
💪

The muscle grows

More protein synthesis means larger fibres — hypertrophy you can measure.

→

This ribosome-biogenesis step sits upstream of the better-known mTOR signalling story, and it is where much of our lab's mechanistic work is focused — in both human muscle biopsies and pre-clinical overload models.

Ferdinand von Walden

MD, PhD · Docent · Principal Investigator
Dept. of Women's & Children's Health, Division of Paediatric Neurology, Karolinska Institutet · Clinician-scientist (paediatrics), Astrid Lindgren Children's Hospital

Ferdinand is a clinician-scientist trained at Karolinska Institutet (MD 2009, PhD 2015), appointed Docent in 2025. His work bridges human exercise physiology and the molecular biology of muscle growth, with a translational focus on muscle in children with cerebral palsy. He combines clinical practice in paediatrics with a laboratory programme on ribosome biogenesis and muscle adaptation.
