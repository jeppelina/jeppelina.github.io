Social distance

Measured across a whole population, one can see it as the structure of social boundaries.

Questions about who accepts whom are old in social science. Bogardus built a scale in the 1920s, from “exclude from the country” to “accept by partnering”. The scale measures one person: how close they will let another group come, and partnering sits at the closest, most demanding rung. [click] Now measure that across a whole population: the pattern of who partners across which lines is the structure of social boundaries. Where few partner across a line the boundary is strong; where many do, it is weaker. This is why partner choice has long been read as an indicator of integration and assimilation, Gordon, and later Alba and Nee.

Two mechanisms

Structure Who is available to partner with: group sizes, age, and where people live.


Assortativity the tendency to partner within group, estimated net of structure.

Define the two mechanisms. Structure: the pool. Assortativity: the within-group tendency net of the pool. Observed rate is both. Flag the terminology: assortativity, not preference. Reason comes at the limitation slide.

METHOD

Measuring the boundary

The pivot to method. I have described what I study and why. Now, how. Pause on this slide.

Register data is wild

Sweden has one of the most complete population records anywhere, reaching back to the church parish registers in the 1600s.

  • Every resident has a personal identity number.
  • The number is recorded wherever a person appears: birth, school, employment, income, medical care, housing, family ties.
  • The registers link all of it. Residence is recorded to a 100-metre square.
  • Accessed through a secure server at statistics Sweden, with strict confidentiality rules.

Not a survey: no questionnaire, no recall error.

The records run from the parish books of the 1600s and 1700s to the present. The personal identity number ties together every place a person is registered: school, work, healthcare, housing, taxes. Geocoded to a 100-metre square. Full population, every year.

The key restriction: every ego is a person of Swedish ancestry, born in Sweden to two Swedish-born parents. The question is how their partner choice varies with the alter, so the alters are the ones that span every origin and immigrant generation. Two estimation files by ego sex, estimated separately: about 719,000 unions with a woman as ego, 733,000 with a man. A union is identified retrospectively, by tracing addresses back to the first shared residence. Three decades are compared: the 1990s, the 2000s, the 2010s. Each ego’s choice set is the real partner plus 30 counterfactual alters, single and age-eligible, same county and year. For each alter we code ancestry, built from country of birth (the alter’s own and their parents’), then grouped into twelve origin regions; generation, with the first generation split by adult versus child arrival at the age-10 cutoff, plus the Sweden-born second generation; the age gap; education homogamy at four levels; and residential distance. The honest caveat: ancestry here is country of birth, not self-identified identity. How non-Swedish egos chose is the companion paper.

Conditional logit

Union formation is modelled as a discrete choice of partner.

P(y_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\exp(\textcolor{#13386B}{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_{ij})}{\sum_{k \in \textcolor{#13386B}{C_i}}\, \exp(\textcolor{#13386B}{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_{ik})}

C_iThe choice set: every single available in that county and year. The partner market sits inside the model.

\betaWhat is left once that market is conditioned out.

So β estimates assortativity as we observe it: the within-group tendency, net of who was available.

One model, one point. The room has seen this before, so do not dwell on the algebra. Numerator: the couple that formed. Denominator: the choice set, every single actually present in that county and year. Because the partner market is in the denominator, the model conditions on it. Distance enters as a covariate too. So the ancestry coefficient is not the raw endogamy rate. It is the within-group tendency net of who was available: assortativity as we observe it. Land that, then move to the decomposition.

The decomposition

From the fitted model we can predict: apply its estimates back to the data and calculate partnering rates.

Keep the slide simple: from the fitted model we can predict, calculating the partnering rate the estimates imply for the data. Then talk around the 2x2. It is one model, not three: every attribute is interacted with period, so the single fit carries decade-specific coefficients. The diagonal is observed, each decade’s data at its own period. The off-diagonal is counterfactual: a decade’s market, but the other decade’s coefficients, which I get by recoding the period dummies. Down the left is the structure effect, the market changing; along the bottom is the assortativity effect. They sum to the observed change. The results slides walk the numbers.

Implications for assimilation theory

Straight-line assimilation expects ethnic boundaries to blur as groups converge: intermarriage rises because the boundary itself weakens (Gordon; Alba and Nee).

  • What we find instead. Net of the partner market, the assortativity component did not soften, and for several origin groups it hardened.
  • This is the tension with the theory. The rise in intermarriage is real, but structural: a diversifying market. The boundary itself, the thing assimilation predicts should weaken, did not.
  • And a group is not a fixed population. Iranian-origin Swedes in 1990 are not the same people as in 2019. Continued arrival keeps refilling the first generation, so a repeated cross-section can mask assimilation happening within cohorts.

The first discussion slide, engaged directly with the theory. Straight-line assimilation, Gordon and then Alba and Nee, makes a directional prediction: as groups converge the boundary between them weakens, and intermarriage rises because of that weakening. Our decomposition splits the intermarriage rise into structure and assortativity. The rise is real, but net of the diversifying market the assortativity component did not soften, and for several groups it hardened. That is the tension: assimilation theory predicts the boundary itself weakens, and in our estimates it did not. The honest caveat: a group is not a fixed population. Iranian-origin Swedes in 1990 are an early cohort; by 2019 the label covers different people, later arrivals and a growing second generation. Continued immigration keeps refilling the first generation, so a flat coefficient for the labelled group can still sit on top of each cohort assimilating. Ours is a repeated cross-section, not a cohort design.

What is in each component

Structure is what the model conditions on:

  • the single population in that county and year
  • group sizes
  • age-eligibility
  • residential distance

Assortativity is the residual left over:

  • a genuine tendency to partner within group
  • the other side’s reciprocal acceptance
  • norms and third-party influence
  • structure the model did not measure, and meeting contexts it cannot see (online dating)

A residual collects everything not modelled. That is why we report it as assortativity, not preference.

The second discussion slide. Be precise about what each term contains. Structure is what the choice set controls for. Assortativity is the residual, and a residual is not a clean measure of preference. State the honest accounting.

The opening question

What the room predicted at the start.

You were asked whether Swedes have become more or less open.

  • Swedes do partner across ethnic lines more often now.
  • But the decomposition attributes almost all of it to structure: the partner market became more diverse.
  • Net of the market, the assortativity component did not soften.

We mix more, but mostly because the market changed, not because openness did.

The callback. Bring the room back to its opening vote, now beside the finding. The observed increase is real. The decomposition attributes almost all of it to the changing market. Net of that, assortativity did not soften. The mechanism behind the residual remains open, which is the next slide.

A limitation and avenue for future: homophily or hierarchy

About a minute. A measured rise in the assortativity component does not identify its mechanism. The same observed within-group partnering can come from homophily, where each group sorts toward its own, or from hierarchy, where one group is selective and the rest pair within by exclusion. The realised matches are identical. This is why the talk reports assortativity, not preference.
