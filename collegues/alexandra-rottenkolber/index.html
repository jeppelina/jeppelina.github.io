Research Occupational gender segregation

About the work

Her dissertation asks how occupational gender segregation emerges and how it reproduces itself, the slow, structural process by which workers end up sorted into different kinds of jobs.

The starting point is simple: the careers people imagine for themselves are shaped by the people they already know.

Working at the Institute for Analytical Sociology, Alexandra combines Swedish full-population register data with social network analysis and text analysis. That pairing lets her follow whole cohorts of workers and the connections between them, rather than relying on survey snapshots.

She studies how personal networks, exposure to peers and family, and the boundaries that networks draw around groups shape gendered occupational mobility, the routes by which a person moves into, out of, or between occupations over a working life.

The aim is an account of segregation grounded in mechanism: not only measuring the gap between fields, but explaining the everyday social processes that keep producing it.

    Methods How the questions get answered

    Toolkit

    Alexandra works across computational methods, picking the tool that fits the mechanism rather than the other way around. Most of her analysis runs in two languages.

    Languages
    Python & R
    • /01 Social network analysis Mapping the ties between people, and the boundaries those ties draw, to see how exposure to peers and family steers occupational choices.
    • /02 Natural language processing Reading large text collections at scale to surface patterns of language, attention and openness that numbers alone would miss.
    • /03 Machine learning Detecting structure and prediction signals in high-dimensional register and network data.
    • /04 Agent-based modelling Simulating how individual decisions add up to large-scale segregation, a way to test which mechanisms can actually produce the patterns observed in the data.
    • /05 Statistics The common ground beneath it all: careful estimation and inference on population-scale data.
