About & research

A sociologist who works with text at scale

Simon Walo is a postdoctoral researcher at the Swedish Excellence Centre for Computational Social Science (SweCSS) and the Institute for Analytical Sociology (IAS) at Linköping University, which he joined through the SweCSS Visiting Fellows Program. He completed his PhD in sociology at the University of Zurich, in the Department of Sociology at the chair of Professor Jörg Rössel. Originally from Switzerland, he describes the Linköping postdoc as completing his transition into computational social science.

Three sociologies

His work spans the sociology of work, the sociology of culture, and the sociology of science: how people judge their own jobs, how cultural meanings differ across Europe, and how scientific knowledge takes shape.

NLP meets networks

He does computational social science at the intersection of natural language processing and network analysis, treating large text collections as data and turning the relationships inside them into structure that can be measured.

A network of causal claims

His current project extracts causal claims from a large corpus of scientific abstracts to build a network of causal relationships, examining patterns of scientific innovation and how academic knowledge spreads.

Featured study

Testing the “bullshit jobs” theory

“Bullshit” after all? Why people consider their jobs socially useless.

Work, Employment and Society, 2023

1,811 US workers analysed
2× higher odds in finance & sales

The first quantitative test of a popular theory

The anthropologist David Graeber argued that a large share of modern jobs are, in the workers' own eyes, socially useless. The idea spread widely, but it had never been tested with data. Simon's study was the first quantitative test of the “bullshit jobs” theory.

Analysing 1,811 US workers, the study found that people working in finance and sales were more than twice as likely to consider their own jobs socially useless. The paper drew wide media coverage and brought careful evidence to a debate that had run mostly on anecdote.

A short personal note

Beyond the research

Simon has a soft spot for music. He has co-organised a music festival and still goes to concerts whenever he can, which is a good reminder that the patterns he studies in text also play out in the rooms where people gather.

Alongside research, he has taught university seminars on Utopian Societies, Social Movements, and the Sociology of Work, and he built an interactive web app, hosted as a Hugging Face Space, that visualises semantic change in word embeddings, the same idea that runs through the schematic above.
