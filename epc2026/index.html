The work behind the poster

The opportunity structure of educational homogamy

Educational homogamy is one of the most consistent findings in family demography: people with higher education tend to partner with each other. Behind that simple pattern sits a question that has outlasted decades of research. Is it about who we choose, or about who we get to meet?

Using Swedish population registers covering every first union from 1991 to 2022, we decomposed educational homogamy into its structural components: residential proximity, shared workplaces, and shared university enrolment. For couples where both partners hold tertiary education, those opportunity structures together explain 29 to 40 percent of their homogamy, with a clean dose-response pattern: each additional year of shared employer or university overlap substantially raises the odds of a union. For the least educated, the same channels explain almost nothing.

This paper extends an earlier study with Ben Jarvis ("However Far Away?"), which found that residential proximity alone mediates 20 to 40 percent of ethnic endogamy in Sweden. Here we add workplaces and universities as channels, and switch the dimension of stratification from ethnicity to education. The result is a clearer picture of how spatial and institutional sorting underwrite seemingly elective patterns of pairing, and how that underwriting varies sharply across the education distribution.

One line takeaway. Universities and workplaces sort the highly educated into homogeneous pools long before the moment we usually call partner choice. The least educated sort themselves through channels we cannot see in registers.
Where this fits

A research agenda on boundaries and choice

The dissertation behind this poster asks one question in four ways: how much of observed partner sorting is opportunity, and how much is residual assortativity, across ethnic and educational lines, in registers covering the full Swedish population, using counterfactual partner comparisons. A fifth paper, on cohabitation and mortality, predates this thread but uses the same register infrastructure.

The bigger story

Rising intermarriage. Stable boundaries.

Sweden's immigrant population has grown dramatically since 1991. Ethnic diversity has reshaped the partner market. Over the same period, interethnic unions have become more common in absolute terms. That reads like integration: boundaries eroding, communities mixing, demographic progress.

When you decompose the trend, the picture inverts.

Conditional logit models that hold partner availability constant show that the residual assortativity for same-group partners has not weakened. In several configurations, particularly among Swedish-origin women, it has strengthened. The rise in interethnic unions is not a sign of softening boundaries. It is a mechanical consequence of a partner market reshaped by migration and segregation. More diversity makes same-group pairing structurally harder, even when the appetite for it is unchanged or stronger.

"Without accounting for who is actually available, we mistake a structural shift for an attitudinal one. The Swedish case shows you cannot read boundary change off mixing rates alone."

This matters for how demographic trends get interpreted. Rising intermarriage is routinely read as evidence that integration is working. Without an opportunity-side counterfactual, that reading is unsafe. The same observed rise can come from openness or from arithmetic, and the policy implications differ.

The poster paper upstairs is the methodological cousin of this one: same logic, different dimension, same insistence that opportunity be modelled before behaviour gets named.

Play with it

Three small simulations

The opportunity-versus-assortativity distinction is easier to feel than to explain. Three browser simulations make the lever visible. Adjust segregation, in-group attraction, and distance sensitivity, then watch couples form and see how much sorting emerges from each.

  • Space or preference
    Two groups in a city. Crank segregation, leave attraction at zero, watch endogamy rise from geography alone.
  • Structure or behaviour
    Compare two snapshots. See how rising exogamy can come from compositional change while behaviour stays constant.
  • Status or homophily
    Three groups with directed preferences. The same endogamy pattern can come from "I want my own" or from "others exclude me".
